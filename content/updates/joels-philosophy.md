Having been recently named as head coach, I feel it fair to let you know a bit about my philosophy toward sport. I believe youth sports should help young people become more capable, confident and resilient through the medium of sport. My goal is to help athletes discover what they are capable of, learn how to work through challenges, and build a lifelong love of being active and outdoors. Mountain biking is the medium; developing character is the mission.

This mission is embodied in Olympism, the philosophy of the Olympic Movement, succinctly captured in the Olympic creed, “The important thing in life is not the triumph, but the fight; the essential thing is not to have won, but to have fought well.”

Pierre de Coubertin believed in Olympism as, "a philosophy of life, exalting and combining in a balanced whole the qualities of body, will and mind. Blending sport with culture and education, Olympism seeks to create a way of life based on the joy of effort, the educational value of good example, social responsibility and respect for universal fundamental ethical principles."

Olympism is bringing sport, culture and education together to develop body, will and mind. Sport serves a purpose far beyond results. The effort we give, the example we set, and the respect we show…this matters as much or more than the finish line.

Mountain biking takes this philosophy and turns the volume up to 11. Instead of a predictable court or playing field, we ride through forests, on hillsides, and in rain, cold, heat, mud and dust. No two trails are the same, and even a familiar trail can change from one ride to the next. Every ride offers a chance to adapt, solve a problem and learn something about ourselves.

“Citius – Altius – Fortius” — Faster, Higher, Stronger, the original Olympic motto captures how I think about this process of improvement. It is not simply being better than someone else, but becoming more capable than we were yesterday. Competition gives us a chance to test ourselves, but a result is only one measure of progress. Asking a good question, riding a difficult section with more confidence or responding to a setback with patience is just as meaningful.

These lessons become tangible on the trail. Learning to care for a bike calls for curiosity. Working through a mechanical problem calls for patience and resourcefulness. A difficult corner teaches humility: recognizing our limits, asking for guidance and practicing before adding speed. Confidence grows through preparation and small successes, not pressure to take unnecessary risks. With perseverance, resilience and courage, riders can gradually redefine what they believe is possible.

My responsibility is to create an environment where athletes feel supported, challenged and welcome, whatever their starting point. I want us to work hard, celebrate progress, support our teammates, and treat other riders and the trails with respect. Success means athletes leaving our program with stronger character, greater confidence and resilience, and a lasting love of riding. If they carry those qualities into the rest of their lives, we have accomplished something far more important than a race result.
